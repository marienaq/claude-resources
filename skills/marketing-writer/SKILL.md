## How Marketing Content Differs from Learning Content

Learning content assumes the reader opted in. They're in the course, they're reading the guide. Marketing content earns attention in a feed where the reader is already scrolling past.

| Learning content | Marketing content |
|-----------------|-------------------|
| Context first, then the point | Point of view first, then context |
| Teach, then practice | Provoke, then earn the right to explain |
| Reader is committed | Reader is deciding whether to keep reading |
| Value is in the depth | Value is in the angle |
| Headers organize content | Every line earns the next line |

## Voice and Anti-Patterns

All voice and style rules: `/writing-guide.md` at the project root (canonical). Mariena's full voice profile, signature moves, anti-patterns, word swaps, and edit checklist live there. Load that file when invoking this skill. The anti-patterns matter even more in marketing because the platforms where this content lives are saturated with the exact cliches Mariena avoids.

This skill adds only the marketing-specific anti-patterns:

- **The testimonial opening.** "I attended X and loved it" reads as a review, not a point of view. Lead with what you think, use the experience as evidence.
- **The credentials lead.** "As someone who has spent 10 years..." or "Excited to announce..." is about you, not about the reader's problem.
- **Stacking CTAs.** Don't ask them to like, comment, share, AND DM. One action.
- **Generic provocation.** "AI is changing everything" stops no one. "Your AI power users are creating marginal impact" stops the right person. Specificity is the scroll-stopper.
- **The "X is the point, not the Y" construction.** MQ, 2026-09-15, on a proposed close ("the recipe is the point, not the consultant"): "AI slop, doesn't sound like me." Same family as the coined phrase below: a symmetrical contrast that packages the idea instead of saying it. When a draft reaches for it, say the thing directly in one clause, or use her aside register ("Not necessarily me.").
- **Coined phrases.** Do not invent a formulation and hand it to her as the thesis. "Foundational training creates demand and no supply" is neat, symmetrical, and not how she thinks. She reasons in business terms the reader already owns: cost, ROI, what compounds, what leaves when the vendor does. A coinage asks the reader to learn your vocabulary before they can agree with you, and it reads as consultant packaging. State the argument in the reader's economics instead. If a phrase is genuinely doing work, she will coin it herself.

## Mariena's patterns on LinkedIn

Observed while editing her drafts, not asserted from theory. Each is marked with how much evidence stands behind it. **Update these as evidence accumulates. Do not treat them as settled.**

### Corroborated (seen across more than one piece)

**Her close is her strongest writing, and it usually holds the thesis.**
On L1 her own closing line beat the one the brief had planned for it. On L0 the review's verdict was "the close is the best writing here, do not touch it." Two independent pieces, same finding.

*How to use it:* read her close first and work backward. It usually tells you what the piece is actually about, which is often not what the opening claims. Treat it as source material, not as the last thing to polish.

**She cushions the tension.**
L1's first draft gave away "I now have a much richer program" in the opening, ahead of the mistakes it was supposed to set up. L0 buried its point under four timestamps of chronology. The Mellonmail reintroduction draft (8/30) opened "Six months ago I witnessed a woman... change how she saw AI," which is the ending of the scene, in the first line. Three independent pieces, three mechanisms, one instinct: reassure the reader early that this turns out fine.

*Treat this as a reliable predictor rather than a tendency.* On any draft of hers, check the first sentence for the payoff before checking anything else. The fix is almost always to open inside the resistance and let the change arrive on its own.

*How to use it:* most structural edits will be some version of letting the discomfort sit longer. Look for the payoff stated before the tension, and for a reassuring clause at the very end that softens honesty she already earned. L1's first draft closed on "working with clients I share values with," which undercut the whole post.

**The warmth lives in her asides.**
"(wait, what?! yes. it's true)" was hers, added mid-revision, and it is the most human thing in the post. L0's "ya know?" was flagged in review as doing real work.

*How to use it:* these are **emotional** parentheticals, not the definitional ones in `/writing-guide.md` §2.11, and the bloat guardrail there does not apply to them. Do not trim them for tightness. When a draft of hers feels flat, the absence of one is often why.

### Provisional (one session, watch for repeats)

**Her self-diagnosis about sounding "impersonal" is wrong in a specific and useful way.**
She says her drafts read as impersonal. Her tone is already warm. What is missing is **people**. No amount of casual phrasing fixed this on L1. One concrete scene did.

*How to use it:* when she asks for a more personable voice, do not add contractions or loosen the register. Ask for a moment she actually watched happen, and place it where it does structural work rather than as decoration. This is the highest-leverage note in this section.

**She detects problems reliably. Her proposed fixes need interrogating.**
Three times on L1 she said something was off: a mistake and its fix that did not match, a close that stopped working after a restructure, a list item running long against the sentence budget. All three diagnoses were correct. Two of the fixes she reached for first were not.

*How to use it:* accept the diagnosis immediately and without argument, then find the real problem before implementing her fix. The homework mistake became the representation mistake this way, and it was the largest single improvement across six versions.

**She retreats from concrete to abstract when a reference makes her nervous.**
"Seating chart" became "rigid early rules" because she worried the image pointed too far back in the post.

*How to use it:* name the trade when you see it. The answer to "this reference sits too far away" is a nearer concrete image, never a vaguer phrase.

**She re-warms a draft after it has been tightened.** Handed an edited version of her own piece, she adds warmth back on her revision pass. On the Mellonmail reintroduction (8/30 to 8/31) she took a tightened draft and added emoji, "HBD to me!", "but say it with me *'✨ It doesn't have to be that way ✨'*", "I may be reaching, but I felt like it instead gave her super powers," and restored a second CTA that the review had asked her to cut.

This is the corollary to "the warmth lives in her asides," and it is the same instinct seen from the other direction: an edit pass reads to her as having removed something human, and she puts it back.

*How to use it:* expect it, and do not read it as her ignoring the review. Some of what returns will trip a guide rule ("super powers" is on the §3.1 hype list; the restored second CTA is stacking). **Flag each once, plainly, then leave it.** Raising the same item on a second pass after she has deliberately put it back is re-litigating a call that is hers. Note it in the open-calls list instead so the record shows it was seen.

### Process, not voice

**She finds the real answer by talking, not by editing.**
The biggest improvement across six versions came from her thinking out loud about why something felt wrong. She arrived at the better mistake mid-paragraph, in her own words, with no options offered to her.

*How to use it:* when she says something is off and cannot yet say why, ask her to talk it through instead of proposing alternatives. Her own drafting briefs already recommend a voice memo before opening a document, for the same reason.

## Mariena's patterns on peer and community channels (Slack, forums)

Seeded from one comparison so far: her edit of the `/cross-post` Fractionals United draft against the original (8/26). One data point. Update as more channels and pieces come in, the same way the LinkedIn section above does.

### Provisional (one comparison, watch for repeats)

**She opens on a standing pattern, not a single dated anecdote, even where the source material would support one.** The draft opened "Six months after a client's foundational AI training..." (tied to one engagement, which the content-plan's own posture notes explicitly allow: "in one program I ran"). Her edit: "I still see most people who only do a foundational AI training using Copilot..." (present tense, an ongoing practitioner observation, no single client anchor).

*How to use it:* in a peer/practitioner channel, default the opener to "I still see..." / "I keep seeing..." pattern language rather than anchoring to one client's timeline, even when a single-client framing is available and safe to use.

**She cuts an inserted analytical aside rather than editing it.** The draft added a clause naming the business-model implication: "Those are different problems, and most engagements are priced to solve the first one." She removed it entirely rather than tightening it.

*How to use it:* in a short peer-channel post, a one-clause "and here's what that implies" gloss reads as over-written. Let the observation stand on its own (here, the "I can see it" vs. "I can build it" contrast) without adding the analytical layer on top. This may be scroll-length pressure rather than a standing preference; watch whether it recurs in longer peer pieces.

**She trades declarative, editorial framing for hedged first-person framing in this register.** "the honest ceiling on what an internal advanced person can do" (a real signature move elsewhere in this series, naming a hard limit plainly) became "what I think is reasonable skills for an advanced internal AI operator."

*How to use it:* the "name the ceiling honestly" move still belongs in LinkedIn Articles and newsletter issues. In a quick peer share, soften the same content to "what I think," which reads as one operator's take rather than a settled verdict.

**She translates the project's own working vocabulary into plainer language, even terms used constantly in the internal docs.** "capability-building work," which appears throughout `content-plan.md` and `task-list.md` as standard shorthand, became "how to build AI skills in their org" in her own voice.

*How to use it:* a term being safe, established project vocabulary does not make it her voice. Internal planning shorthand (capability-building, allocation, tier model) needs translating into plain language every time it surfaces in a draft meant to sound like her, not just on the first pass.

**She isolates the link/CTA on its own final line rather than folding it into the last content sentence.** The draft ended "...without a tech team behind them. Full thing here if useful to anyone else running capability-building work: [link]" as one sentence. Her edit breaks the CTA into its own paragraph: "The full post is here if useful to anyone else thinking about how to build AI skills in their org: [link]."

*How to use it:* default a short post's CTA to its own final line or paragraph, not fused onto the end of the last content sentence, even in a tight 3-6 sentence format.

## The Scroll Rule

LinkedIn shows roughly three lines before "...see more." Other platforms have similar thresholds. The first 1-2 sentences must give the reader a reason to care that is specific to their situation.

Conference names, personal backstory, and credentials come after the hook, not before.

## Structure: Tension, Evidence, Reframe, Context, CTA

Not every post needs all five sections. But when the content is substantial (conference promos, thought leadership, value prop posts), this is the arc:

### Tension (1-2 sentences)
- Name a problem the audience owns
- Be specific enough that the right reader stops scrolling
- Honest escalation, not hype ("And that's the best case" not "This changes everything")

### Evidence (1-3 sentences)
- Research, data, or observable pattern that validates the tension
- Must land before most readers drop off (within first 5-6 lines)
- Name the source. Vague "studies show" undermines credibility.

### Reframe (2-4 sentences)
- Your point of view on what to do about it
- This is where Mariena's signature moves live: the reframe, the clarifying statement
- Make the abstract concrete (not "team impact" but "training peers, spotting workflows, building shared practices")

### Context (2-4 sentences)
- Why you're saying this now (event, experience, conversation)
- Personal experience as proof of values, not as the hook
- Keep it proportional. The context earns warmth, not word count.

### CTA (1-2 sentences)
- Low-pressure, direct
- One action (a question to answer, a link to read, an invitation to DM)
- No stacking CTAs (don't ask them to like, comment, share, AND DM)
- **On LinkedIn, the CTA invites; it never gates** (ruled 2026-09-14). "DM me if you want to compare notes" is an invitation. "DM me for the deck," "comment YES for the guide," "like for the PDF" gate a resource behind an action, and LinkedIn's ranking model reads that as engagement bait and holds the post to direct connections. Never write the gated form. If there is a resource, put the link in the body. Current platform rules, with sources and dates: `marketing/linkedin-distribution-mechanics.md`; read it before drafting anything bound for LinkedIn.

## Alternative Structure: The Observational Field Note

Not every strong post starts with tension. Mariena's natural default is the field note: lead with something she noticed in the world (a place, a moment, a pattern), then turn it into a point about the reader's work. When the observation is vivid and the turn is earned, this can outperform the tension-first arc, because a concrete scene stops the scroll better than a claim does, and it reads as authentically her.

**The arc:**
- **Observation (2 to 4 sentences).** A concrete, specific scene. The detail is the hook. (Sevilla built for the heat, a maze of narrow streets, the city closing at 2:30.)
- **The turn.** The one line that pivots from the scene to the idea. This is where the field note earns its place. ("Organizations are in their own heat wave right now.")
- **The point.** Your POV on the reader's problem, carried by the metaphor the observation set up.
- **The forward move and CTA.** Same as the main structure.

**The test that keeps it marketing, not a travel diary:** the observation has to be a Trojan horse for a business insight the reader can use. If you could delete the "here's what this means for your work" turn and still have a complete post, it's a diary, not marketing. The scene serves the point. It isn't the point.

**Watch for:** over-narrating the personal ("I did this, then I felt that, then I realized..."). Keep the scene economical. Lived experience is the spine, not the subject. The field note leans closer to the casual line than other formats (see writing guide §1.2, approachable-not-casual), so keep the observation tight and let the insight carry the weight.

## Signature Moves (Marketing Applications)

These patterns from Mariena's voice (full definitions in `/writing-guide.md` §2) work especially well in marketing content:

### The Reframe
Elevate the topic from obvious to interesting.

| Obvious | Reframe |
|---------|---------|
| "Train your employees on AI" | "Turn your AI nerds into team multipliers" |
| "AI adoption is important" | "Individual AI use creates marginal impact. Team impact is the lever." |

### The Clarifying Statement
"This is about X, not Y" sharpens positioning and separates you from the noise.

> "AI starts closing the access gap instead of widening it."

### Punchy Bullets
Fragment bullets land harder than full sentences. Use when listing observable patterns or consequences.

> - Mixed proficiency levels creating friction
> - Inconsistent ways of working across the team
> - The stress of falling behind peers

### The Honest Acknowledgment
Name the real situation without blame. This builds trust faster than claiming expertise.

> "Genuine conversations, not badge scans."

### Tactics that build the relationship, not just the argument

A family of moves that cost credibility to fake and earn it when they are real. **Not a checklist.** Most pieces carry one at most, and a piece with three reads as performed candor, which is worse than none. The list is open; these are the ones observed so far.

- **Say the uncomfortable true thing, without offering a fix.** *"And of course you'll have a couple of stragglers. People that struggle with change, new technology, or just plain hate AI and want nothing to do with it."* Warmer than the Honest Acknowledgment because it declines to solve what it names.
- **Argue against your own interest.** Name yourself as the interested party, then set your own fee aside as the smaller cost. *"I'm not talking so much about the cost of hiring someone awesome like me to teach workshops. I'm talking about the time it takes your people."*
- **Recommend the thing you do not sell.** Not a hedge at the bottom. Enough weight that a reader who picks it feels well served.
- **Vulnerability about the work.** The mistake belongs to the design she owns (see `/writing-guide.md` §13.1), which is what makes it publishable rather than reckless.

**The test:** would this sentence cost her something if it were not true? If the answer is no, it is a credibility gesture rather than a credibility move, and it will read that way.

## Common Marketing Content Types

### LinkedIn Post (Conference / Event)
- Lead with your point of view, not "Excited to announce I'm attending..."
- Use the event as context that earns the post, not as the post itself
- Specificity is the scroll-stopper ("Your AI power users are creating marginal impact" not "AI is changing the workplace")

### LinkedIn Post (Thought Leadership)
- One idea per post. If you're making two points, write two posts.
- Ground the idea in something observable (research, client pattern, workshop moment), not abstract belief
- End with an invitation, not a lecture

### Speaking Proposal / Abstract
- Lead with the audience problem, not your credentials
- Name what attendees will walk away thinking differently about
- Keep it under 150 words unless the submission requires more

### Newsletter / Website Copy
- Same scroll rule applies: first line earns the second
- For website copy, every paragraph should survive the "so what?" test on its own
- For newsletters, the intro paragraph is the post. Everything after it is bonus.

## The review protocol

Run in this order. **Order matters:** the structural checks change what the line edits should be, so line-editing first wastes the work.

Read **Mariena's patterns on LinkedIn** above before starting. It predicts where the problems will be in her drafts specifically, which is faster than finding them cold.

### Before step 1: if the draft diverges from a brief, test the divergence

When Mariena's draft departs from a brief, an outline, or a plan, **the brief is the more likely thing to be wrong.** It was written before anyone tried to say the thing out loud. Drafting is where the real argument gets found.

Do not open a review with "this is different from the plan." That frames her instinct as drift and makes her argue for her own draft. Work out whether the divergence is better first, then report the comparison.

Three questions:

- **Is the new framing more legible to the actual reader?** A more differentiated argument that lands slower can still lose to a plainer one that lands immediately.
- **Does it resolve a problem the brief had?** Briefs that ration a good idea across two pieces, or negotiate with themselves in the ripple-effects section, are telling you the plan was wrong.
- **What survives either way?** Name the lines that carry forward so a spine change does not read as a total loss.

If the divergence wins, rewrite the brief around it and say so. If the brief wins, say why in terms of the reader, not in terms of fidelity to the plan.

### Before step 1: name the shape of every claim before you rule on it

Her claims are usually comparative or longitudinal, and the evidence that settles them is not the evidence in front of you. "Still the hardest," "hasn't moved," "the one that didn't improve," and "more than anything else" are all claims about **change**, and a single time point cannot confirm or refute any of them.

Sort each factual claim into level, change, ranking, or cause. Then go find the evidence that matches the shape. **Never rule a claim unsupported from one time point, one survey, or one export.** Find the baseline first, and if there is no baseline, say the claim is unverifiable rather than saying it is wrong.

*Where this came from (2026-09-09):* a draft rested on "finding where to use AI was the hardest skill." The reviewer checked September self-ratings, found opportunity spotting scoring above most technical skills, and told her the data contradicted her. It did not. The claim was about movement from a December baseline, and against that baseline the skill had improved least of fourteen. The correction came from MQ, not from the review, and the wrong version would have killed the strongest insight in the piece.

### Before step 1: in a series, read the siblings before ruling on the spine

If the piece belongs to a multi-post series, read the sibling posts drawn from the same story bank before ruling on its spine. A new data point is not a new idea, and the collision is invisible when you review one file at a time (L12 review trail, 2026-09-09).

### 1. Safety pass. First, and even on a half-finished draft.

Full rules in `/writing-guide.md` §13. In short: is every mistake the writer's own, does any counterparty (including an implied one) sit in the frame of a failure, is anything identifying, and does any figure need clearance.

This runs first because a beautifully edited paragraph that cannot publish is wasted work, and because the fix is usually structural. Watch specifically for the sentence that trails off and lets the reader supply the ending.

**Before ruling on any of it, search the repo for earlier treatments of the same story or the same client.** Two minutes of `grep` and a look through the relevant `marketing/` subfolders. Old outlines, unsent drafts, published pieces, and per-piece briefs frequently contain a de-identification precedent Mariena set herself, which beats one invented now, and they sometimes change what the piece is about.

*Where this came from (2026-08-30):* a reintroduction issue drew on a client scene that looked like a fresh anecdote. `marketing/newsletters/2026-03-visualization-unlock/outline.md`, five months old and never sent, turned out to be the same client and the same scene, already deliberately abstracted into an archetype with a note to "walk through the general approach without revealing client specifics." That precedent settled the anonymity question, and the existence of an unsent draft on the same idea answered a standing question about why she had stopped writing. It was the highest-value finding of the review and it was one search.

Also check whether the client relationship is still live, because it determines whether clearance is even obtainable. See `/writing-guide.md` §13.5.

### 2. Scroll check

Cover everything below the first three lines. Do those lines earn the click alone? A truism, a setup sentence, or the payoff given away early all fail. Draft several openings and pick; the first one written is almost always setup.

### 3. Arc check

Is the structure earning attention in the right order? Name the job of each paragraph. If two adjacent paragraphs do the same job, one goes even when both are good.

### 4. One idea

If the piece is making two points, it is two pieces. Say which one is stronger and which becomes its own post.

### 5. Claim-to-fix alignment

For any piece that pairs problems with solutions (lessons learned, mistakes, "what I'd do differently"), read each pair in isolation. **Does the fix address the problem the item names?**

A mismatch almost always means the real mistake is one level up from the one written down. Finding it is the highest-value edit available in this whole protocol, and it is invisible unless each pair is read alone. Do not patch the fix to match the claim; go find the actual mistake.

### 6. Does the thesis cover the list?

If the piece closes on a generalization about the items above it, test the generalization against each item. A close that describes one of four items is asserting a pattern the reader cannot see. Either bridge it with one clause that shows the thread, or replace it with the thesis that is actually true of all of them.

### 7. Frame repetition: separate the double-dip from the motif

Repetition is not automatically a defect. Two different things look identical on a first read.

**A double-dip** makes the same move twice. A promise flagged up top ("none of these are about AI") and paid off in the same words at the bottom, with nothing added in between. Keep the stronger placement and give the other something new to do.

**A motif** plants a line early and turns it each time it returns. Each appearance means something the last one did not.

> Planted: readers with big ideas are asking "Can Copilot help with that?"
> Turned: the long tail "stays as an idea, a question, 'Can Copilot do this?', because your people don't have the skills to figure it out."
> Turned again in the close: "Can Copilot do this?" is a skills question, and it is one your people can answer themselves.

Same six words, three different jobs: the reader's question, the diagnosis, the resolution.

**The test:** does the repeat add a turn, or just an echo? If each return recontextualizes the line, it is structure and cutting it would flatten the piece. If it only reminds, cut one.

Motifs are a technique, not a requirement. Most pieces do not need one, and hunting for a motif in a piece that does not have one produces a forced callback.

### 8. Sentence budget and parallelism

For any list of parallel items, count sentences per item. One item at double the length of the others makes the piece sag there, and readers feel it as a loss of momentum even if they cannot name it.

**Count what is structural before setting the budget.** If every item ends with a fix line, a takeaway, or a callback, that line is part of the pattern and the real floor is one higher than the stated rule. Adjust the budget rather than breaking it on three items out of four.

### 9. Voice check

`/writing-guide.md` §1 to §3, plus **Mariena's patterns on LinkedIn** above. Does it sound like Mariena or like LinkedIn? Watch the anti-patterns hardest here; the platforms are saturated with exactly the cliches she avoids. Keep the lines that are unmistakably hers even when a tighter version exists, especially in openings, closes, and asides.

### 10. Line edit

Cut, convert, clarify. `/writing-guide.md` §4. Last, because steps 1 through 9 change what survives.

## Prefer relocation over deletion

When one element is too long, ask whether it wants to be its own beat before cutting it. A scene buried inside a list item, a caveat crammed into a paragraph, or a proof point hiding in a subordinate clause is usually not too long. It is in the wrong place, where it competes with a structure it does not fit.

Pulling it out often satisfies the length rule and improves the piece at the same time, which trimming never does.

## How to report a review

- **Lead with what is working, specifically.** Name the line or move that earns its place, not general encouragement.
- **Structural findings before line edits.** The order of the protocol is the order of the report.
- **For a hook, a close, or any short high-leverage line, give 2 to 3 options with a recommendation** (`/writing-guide.md` §8.1). Mariena picks faster from options than she reacts to a single draft, and options surface tradeoffs a draft hides.
- **Say which calls are hers.** Warmth against tightness, specificity against anonymity, and rhythm preferences are hers to make. Mark them as choices, not corrections.
- **Name the knock-on effects.** If the piece belongs to a series, changing it can invalidate a sibling piece, a shared story bank, or a planned follow-up. Flag those rather than silently letting them contradict.

## Versioning drafts in the file

Keep every version in the working file with a short "what changed and why" under each, plus a list of open calls. Do not overwrite a draft with its successor.

This gives Mariena the reasoning to argue with rather than a finished artifact to accept, lets her lift a line from v2 back into v5, and keeps the record of which tradeoffs were already considered.

**One thesis per file. Split at the moment a second one appears, not after.**

A working file accumulates theses quietly: a draft, a rethink, a second angle that came out of the same evidence. Each looks like a version of the last one and none of them is. Check on every pass whether the file now holds more than one argument, and split it the moment it does, giving each thesis its own file with its own drafts, rulings and open calls.

Two tells that a "version" is actually a separate post: it rests on a different evidence base, or its fix is different. Both were true of L12 v1 on 2026-09-09, which had been sitting in the file as an earlier draft and turned out to be a separate piece drawn from different work, with no clearance exposure at all. It became L14.

*(The original L12 held four theses and reached 31KB before anyone noticed, and the cleanup cost a session.)*

**Newest version at the top.** Current draft first, older versions descending below it, planning and briefing material at the bottom. She reads the file to see the live draft, not the archaeology, and should never scroll past three superseded versions to find the one she asked for. Put the status block at the very top and name which version is current in it.

**Re-read the working file from disk before every pass, and check its modification time.** She edits these files in Obsidian between turns, including renaming and renumbering versions. The copy in context is never authoritative, and a stale copy is most dangerous in exactly the situation where it looks safest: a scripted rewrite that reassembles the whole file.

*The near miss this came from (2026-08-30):* she renumbered her own draft from v2 to v1 and the reviewer's rewrite from v3 to v2 about three minutes before a rebuild script ran against the pre-edit copy. The script only failed because a string assertion happened to miss. Without it, the rebuild would have written a file that erased her renumbering and mislabeled every draft in it.

Practical rule: anchor scripted edits on headings you have just read from disk, assert every anchor string before writing, and prefer targeted edits over full-file reassembly when the file has been open in Obsidian.

## The three edit tests

Before finalizing:

1. **Cover the bottom two-thirds.** Does the top third earn a click on its own?
2. **Read the first sentence in a feed of 50 other posts.** Does it sound like everyone else?
3. **Remove the CTA.** Does the piece still have a point of view worth reading? If not, the CTA is doing too much work.
