# Session Brief (Phase 1: define what to teach)

Turn a raw session or course idea into a scoped content brief: the thesis, the litmus that decides what is in, the narrative arc, the techniques or content per stage, the guardrails, and the open build items. This skill defines **what to teach**, not how to deliver it. Delivery design (backward design, the self-paced/workshop blend, session flow) is Phase 2, a separate instructional-design skill that builds on this brief.

## When to use it

- A subject-matter expert (often Mariena) arrives with a new session or course idea and you need to define the content before designing delivery.
- The scope is a whole session or course. For a single teaching unit, use the role-specific-problem-brief skill: it shares this skill's interaction model but has its own agenda and altitude.

Do not use it for: instructional design or activity design, slide/guide/QRG production, or critiquing an existing draft (use `/session-design-review` for that).

## How to work: Interview-led scoping (the interaction model)

This is the heart of the skill. You are not generating the course. You are interviewing the expert to pull the course out of their head and shaping it with them, the way a seasoned learning strategist works. The skill runs on this posture, and it is the same interaction model the role-specific-problem-brief skill uses (shared DNA), even though the two agendas differ.

Posture rules:

1. **Extract, do not fabricate.** The content lives in the expert's head and in materials that already exist. Your job is to surface it, structure it, and pressure-test it, not invent it. When you must propose, mark it clearly as a proposal for them to react to.
2. **Menu, then cut.** For each decision, present a menu of candidates with the actual "how you would do it," plus a recommendation and the reasoning. Let the expert cut and keep. Do not hand them a finished answer.
3. **One decision at a time.** Reflect each decision back in plain words and confirm before baking it into the brief.
4. **Show the why before the vote.** Walk the tradeoffs and recommend before asking the expert to pick.
5. **Keep the brief live.** Update it after each decision. Never let decisions live only in the conversation.

This mirrors the "AI as a thinking partner" thesis: facilitate the expert's thinking, do not do it for them. The skill practices what good course design preaches.

## The interview agenda (nine steps)

Front-load the high-leverage steps. The single most important is Step 4 (thesis and litmus). Do it before selecting any content; skipping it is what makes technique lists feel muddled.

1. **Gather and differentiate.** Pull everything that already exists on the topic (past sessions, research, transcripts, the original course description). Find the nearest sibling and adjacent offerings, and name this session's one distinct, defensible job: what it does that they do not. No overlap is the bar.
2. **Pull audience constraints up front.** Who they are, what fatigues them, what they will dismiss as fluff or obvious, where they will take an escape hatch. Treat these as design constraints, not afterthoughts.
3. **Lock the frame.** Three structured either/or choices before any content: positioning (versus siblings), format or mode (self-paced, VILT, or blended), and depth (how many things, taught deeply versus a survey). These gate everything downstream.
4. **Define the thesis and litmus.** One sentence: what does this session actually teach? Then a filter that decides what belongs and what does not. Sort every later candidate against it. This is the highest-leverage step in the whole process.
5. **Lay the narrative arc.** Organize the content as a workflow or sequence of stages, not a flat menu. The arc is the spine the session hangs on.
6. **Scope stage by stage.** For each stage: restate the learning objective, present a menu of candidate techniques or content with the actual move, run each through the litmus and the audience-dismissal test, keep or cut, then attach its guardrails (how it gets misused) and its "when to reach for it."
7. **Pull cross-cutting elements up as they surface.** Point of view and principles, guardrail patterns, decision trees, disciplines, activity patterns. Do not leave them buried inside one stage.
8. **Defer what needs evidence.** Anything you can validate empirically (does the demo land, does the tool behave) gets marked "TBD after testing" and validated in the learner's real environment. Do not decide on paper what you can test.
9. **Keep a living brief.** Maintain it in a Decided versus Remaining-build-work structure and update after every decision.

## Standing checks (apply throughout)

- **Distinct defensible job.** Every session, and every technique in it, must have a job nothing else in the curriculum already does.
- **Audience-dismissal test.** For each technique or framing, ask: will this audience think it is obvious, fluff, or made up? If yes, it lives or dies on the demo. Flag it.
- **Voice and plain-language check.** On every name and framing: no jargon or consultant-speak, no woo-woo, test against the audience's own words. Follow `/writing-guide.md`.
- **Facilitate, do not fabricate.** If a technique asks AI to infer the substance without context, name the misuse and the fix.

## The output: a living scoping brief

A markdown brief saved in the course or session folder, consultant-facing (a working format, not stakeholder-ready), containing:

- Header block: status, module, format, audience, prerequisites
- Thesis and litmus
- Differentiation (versus siblings)
- Narrative arc
- One section per stage: objective, techniques, guardrails, when-to-use tree
- Cross-cutting point of view, principles, and disciplines
- **Decided** (scope locked) versus **Remaining build work**
