# Session Design Review

Critique and iterate on draft session designs (workshops, training sessions, facilitated meetings) so each moment earns its place and primes the next. This skill captures the moves used when pressure-testing a draft to find lazy framings, narrative arc breaks, talking points landing in the wrong place, activities that surface the wrong data, and openings that prime the wrong frame of mind.

This is a **critique and refinement** skill, not a generation skill. Use the other skills (`/facilitation-guide`, `/presentation-writer`, `/role-specific-problem-brief`) to produce drafts. Use this one to make them sharper.

## When to Use

Use this skill when:
- You have a draft session design and want to pressure-test it before finalizing
- Something feels off about an opening, transition, or full arc, and you can't yet name what
- You're deciding between multiple format options for an activity
- A draft has too many talking points crammed into one section
- An activity is supposed to give participants agency but feels reactive
- You're iterating on a session based on stakeholder or peer feedback

## Core Principles

### 1. Every moment primes the next moment

Before each activity, ask: **"What frame of mind do participants need to be in for what comes next?"** Then check: **"Does the current moment get them there?"**

If the chat opener measures their output (defensiveness) but the next activity asks for honest sharing (vulnerability), the opener fights the activity. Fix the opener.

The session is a sequence of emotional and cognitive states. The design either builds the sequence intentionally or fights it accidentally.

### 2. Talking points belong where they LAND, not where they're "convenient"

A "necessary message" delivered at the wrong moment creates the opposite effect.

"You should have been doing X independently" lands as:
- **Judgment of the past** if delivered before participants share what they've done
- **Forward commitment** if delivered after they've shown lived behavior and seen the framework

The content doesn't change. The timing changes everything. When auditing talking points, ask:
- Does this land as forward or backward at this moment?
- Does it prime openness or defensiveness here?
- Would it land better after the shared-data moment?

### 3. Generative beats reactive for agency moments

When the goal is to give participants ownership of something (a role, a framework, a decision), have them **generate first**, then reveal/compare. Asking them to react to your framework is reactive even if you call it "co-creation."

| Reactive (weak agency) | Generative (real agency) |
|---|---|
| "Here's our framework. What do you think?" | "Put up your version first. Then we'll reveal ours and compare." |
| "Rate each item achievable/stretch/unrealistic" | "Add what's missing from our list." |
| "Vote on these proposed options" | "Generate options, then we'll synthesize." |

The reactive version puts you at the center. The generative version puts participants at the center. Both look like "input" but they create completely different felt experiences.

### 4. Question the default framing for bias

First-draft framings are often lazy or default. Pressure-test by asking: **"What answers will this question actually surface? Is that what we actually need?"**

Common lazy framings and their problems:

| Lazy framing | What it surfaces | What's wrong |
|---|---|---|
| "What's expected of you?" | Recall of what was said to them | Tests memory; primes compliance, not insight |
| "What does the role look like?" | Abstract ideals | Hand-wave language; doesn't ground in reality |
| "How many times did you do X?" | Quantitative measurement | Defensiveness, performance |
| "What surprised you?" (with no anchor) | Vague reactions | Doesn't surface anything actionable |

Stronger framings ground in **lived experience**, **specific decisions**, or **honest emotional reality**, and they signal that what the person actually thinks/feels matters here.

### 5. Match format to the problem

Different activities surface different data. Pick the format that does what you actually need.

| If you need... | Use... | Not... |
|---|---|---|
| Broad input + every voice | 1-2-4-All or anonymous whiteboard | Open chat |
| Focused critique on an individual artifact | Troika consulting (3 people) or pair stress-test | Group discussion |
| Honest collective signal | Anonymous dot voting + comments | Verbal share-out |
| Sense-making after a reveal | Pod discussion with specific prompts | "Any questions?" |
| Co-creation of a framework | Generate → reveal → compare | "Vote on our proposal" |
| Recruiting interest | Multi-select poll + 1:1 follow-up | "Raise your hand if interested" |

If the activity surfaces the wrong data (or asks people to do the wrong cognitive/emotional work), change the format, not the prompt.

### 6. Don't duplicate work across sections

Same question asked twice across sections = bloat. If the AI Action Plan phase asks "what do you need to succeed for each item," don't also ask "what would you need" in an earlier role activity. Different framings of the same question feel redundant and dilute both moments.

When auditing, ask: **"Is this question being asked elsewhere in the session? In a sharper way?"**

### 7. Mind the audience's emotional state at each transition

Map the felt experience moment by moment. What are participants likely feeling at the start of each section?

- Defensive?
- Reflective?
- Engaged?
- Anxious?
- Bored?
- Open?

Each section transition either honors that state or fights it. The harder the message (a tough expectation, a critical assessment, a deadline), the more the moment before it needs to set up receptivity.

If you wouldn't deliver this message to a colleague in their current emotional state, redesign the moment before it, not the message itself.

## Review Process

### Pass 1: End-to-end narrative arc (15-30 min)

Walk through each section in order. For each, ask:
- What's the goal of this section?
- What frame of mind do participants need to be in?
- Does the PRIOR section get them there?
- Does THIS section set up what comes NEXT?

Map the arc on paper. Look for breaks where one section undermines the next.

### Pass 2: Talking point audit

For sections with multiple talking points (especially openings):

1. List each talking point separately
2. For each, identify what it primes
3. Ask: does it belong at THIS moment in the arc, or somewhere else?
4. Reorder, split, or move as needed

Common pattern: 4 talking points crammed into one opening because they "all need to be said." Better solution: split across multiple moments, each landing where it primes well.

### Pass 3: Activity format check

For each generative/discussion activity:
- What does this format actually surface?
- Is that what we need?
- Could a different format surface more useful data?

Common pattern: defaulting to 1-2-4-All everywhere. It's great for broad input but weaker for focused critique. Match the format to the problem.

### Pass 4: Opening question pressure-test

Openings disproportionately set the tone. For the first chat question or activity:
- What frame of mind does this prime?
- Is it the frame we need for what comes next?
- Does it accidentally signal "you're being measured" when we need "you're being heard"?

Often the first 30 seconds of the session need the most attention.

### Pass 5: Timing reality check

Add up the times. Run through the activities in your head with a real audience.

- Does the math work?
- Does any section have implausibly tight time for what's being asked (e.g., "6 pods share in 1 min" = 10 seconds each = not actual surfacing)?
- Where's the buffer if something runs long?
- What can compress without losing the point?

## Quality Checklist

### Narrative arc
- [ ] Does each section prime the next?
- [ ] Are talking points placed where they LAND, not where they're convenient?
- [ ] Do generative activities precede reactive ones when agency matters?
- [ ] Are hard messages placed AFTER shared data, not before?

### Framing
- [ ] Is the opening question reflective rather than measuring?
- [ ] Do activity prompts ground in lived experience, not recall or aspiration?
- [ ] Has each prompt been pressure-tested for what it actually surfaces?

### Format
- [ ] Does each activity surface what you actually need?
- [ ] No duplicate questions across sections?
- [ ] Format matches whether you need broad input, focused critique, individual reflection, etc.?

### Audience
- [ ] Have you mapped emotional state at each transition?
- [ ] Are there moments where participants are likely to feel judged, exposed, or overwhelmed? Have you mitigated?
- [ ] Does the agency moment let participants shape (not just react)?

### Timing
- [ ] Does the math work end-to-end?
- [ ] Are share-out activities budgeted realistically (e.g., 1 min per group minimum)?
- [ ] Is there flex if something runs long? What compresses if needed?

### Stakeholder readiness
- [ ] If a co-facilitator (like {{client lead}}) is delivering hard messages, have they been pre-talked? Are they ready to receive surprise data without defending?
- [ ] If the activity might surface things the org isn't ready to act on, is that named upfront?

## Working with the user

This skill is most useful as a **dialogue**: pressure-testing one thing at a time and iterating. Avoid trying to critique everything at once. Better:

1. Ask which section/moment feels off
2. Apply one or two principles to that moment
3. Propose options with trade-offs
4. Let the user choose
5. Update the section
6. Move to the next

When proposing alternatives, surface 2-4 options with what each would surface and the trade-offs. Recommend one, but commit to the reasoning. Avoid presenting options without a recommendation.

When the user pushes back on your recommendation (or catches something you missed), update your thinking. The work we did in June 2026 on the Champion recommit session restructured the arc 3 times based on user pushback, each iteration sharper than the one before.
